# Scaling Synthetic Robot Data for Pretraining: What Works for Us (and What Doesn't)

TL;DR: Robots don't get trillions of web tokens for free. A robot's "tokens" are joint angles, contacts, camera streams, and physics, so we generate them. We build a lightweight, [Gym-Genesis](https://github.com/huggingface/gym-genesis) pipeline to synthesize large, diverse robot episodes for pretraining vision-language-action (VLA) models like **SmolVLA**. The recipe is simple: [Inverse Kinematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_kinematics) expert controllers inside a fast, vectorized [simulator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robotics_simulator), broad domain randomization, and outputs that plug straight into imitation learning (IL) and reinforcement learning (RL) evaluation.

## Why synthetic data?

Real robot datasets are expensive and narrow. [Synthetic data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_data) lets you create many different versions of the same task as you want, objects in new positions, different colors, lighting changes, camera angles, even slight tweaks to the robot’s motions. There’s no practical limit, unlike the real world, where collecting each new variation takes time, effort, and hardware. The question isn't "How much can we make?" but "What kind of scale and diversity actually helps pretraining?".

**H1.** Large-scale, task-structured synthetic data generated by IK experts improves VLA pretraining; especially when paired with strong visual diversity and calibrated action noise.

We don't claim IK solves sim-to-real transfer. It *does* make it feasible for small teams to explore scale, diversity, and curriculum; without a warehouse of robots.

## What we build

We built [Gym-Genesis](https://github.com/huggingface/gym-genesis), a GPU-vectorized data factory for robot learning. It produces high-quality episodes (images, proprioception, actions) with heavy domain randomization (cameras, lighting, object placement). On cube-stacking tasks we generate ≈5k episodes per task, then use them for imitation pretraining and downstream RL/IL evaluation. The code is deliberately minimal, outputs native PyTorch tensors, and integrates with LeRobotDataset out-of-the-box.

> **Why Genesis?** It's fast, vectorized, and easy to batch. That translates to throughput (scale), stability (fewer sim stalls), and repeatability (clean seeds), exactly what you want in a data factory.

## Cameras
To maximize visual coverage, we used three cameras placed in complementary positions:

- Top camera: provides a global bird’s-eye view of the workspace, so the policy always has access to object locations and overall scene layout.
- Side camera: captures depth cues, occlusions, and contact events that are invisible from the top.
- Wrist camera: mounted on the gripper, it delivers an egocentric view critical for fine alignment during grasping and stacking.

## Visualization & Rendering

We used Genesis in two modes: a rasterizer and a ray tracer.

[Rasterization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rasterisation) projects geometry onto the image plane in a single pass. It’s extremely fast and ideal for generating large datasets—this is what we used for the bulk of our pretraining episodes.

[Ray tracing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_tracing_(graphics)) (via [LuisaRender](https://github.com/LuisaGroup/LuisaRender)) simulates light transport by tracing rays as they bounce across surfaces. This produces softer shadows, reflections, and more realistic lighting effects. The cost is speed, but the gain is images that look much closer to real camera captures.

## The Expert Policy (v2): from Cartesian Waypoints → IK → Joint Interpolation

We use a batched, stage-conditioned, inverse kinematics (IK) controller that outputs smooth 6-DoF joint targets for **[SO-101](https://github.com/TheRobotStudio/SO-ARM100)**. Everything runs vectorized in **Gym-Genesis**, so we avoid per-environment Python loops and keep throughput high. For instance, we can run 24k environments at the same time! By just setting `envs=24000`.

### Setup and notation

* Batch size **B**.
* Joint vector **q ∈ ℝ⁶** (last element **q₆** is the gripper).
* Gripper link frame **L_gripper**; forward kinematics **f_FK(q) ∈ ℝ³**.
* Cube positions from observation: **c₁, c₂ ∈ ℝ^(B×3)**.
* Fixed "tool-down" orientation given by a quaternion from an Euler rotation of **−90°** around **x**:

  **q_tool↓ = Quat(Rₓ(−90°))**


  repeated across the batch.
* Constants that matches the code:

$$
g_{\text{open}} = 0.5, \quad 
g_{\text{closed}} = 0.1, \quad 
z_{\text{off}} = 0.18, \quad 
\delta_z = 0.0981, \quad
\Delta_{xy} =
\begin{bmatrix}
-0.005 \\
\;\;0.02
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

### Why this expert policy works

* **Continuity:** warm-started IK + dense joint blending suppress configuration jumps.
* **Contact timing:** the late gripper ramp avoids pushing the cube before full alignment.
* **Geometry compensation:** stage offsets $(z_{\text{off}}, \Delta_{xy}, \delta_z)$ account for gripper thickness and cube size.
* **Throughput:** all math is batched; Genesis keeps the sim vectorized and stable.

## What helps vs. what doesn't (quick notes)

* **Helps:** Broad camera/lighting domain randomization, small **action noise**, waypoint counts that keep IK feasible, joint-space blending, warm-start IK
* **Hurts (early on):** Aggressive clutter + harsh speculars before policy stabilizes; overly tight lateral offsets; tiny waypoint counts near joint limits

## Scope

This repository is a **data generation toolkit**, not a leaderboard or results paper. The goal is simple: make large-scale, randomized robot pretraining data **fast, scalable, and accessible**—so small teams can run big experiments.
